## Paddy: planthopper and hopper burn

Brown planthopper (BPH) adults and nymphs cluster at the base of paddy tillers, just above
the water line. Early signs are yellowing from the base upwards, drying of tillers in
circular patches ("hopper burn"), and sooty mould on the stem. Check 10 random hills per
field by parting the tillers and looking at the stem base; count hoppers per hill. A
commonly used field threshold is around 5-10 hoppers per hill, but always confirm the local
recommendation before advising a spray. Ask the farmer whether nitrogen was applied
recently, because heavy nitrogen use favours BPH outbreaks. Avoid unnecessary insecticide
sprays that kill natural predators such as spiders and mirid bugs.

## Paddy: blast disease

Blast appears as spindle-shaped (diamond) lesions on leaves with grey centres and brown
margins, and as neck rot at the panicle base. It spreads fastest in continuous wet weather
with high humidity and cool nights. Inspect the leaf blades of 10-20 plants across the
field, record the percentage of leaf area affected, and note whether the field is in the
tillering stage or at panicle emergence (neck blast is the more damaging stage). Ask about
seed source, previous crop and whether the field stays wet for long periods.

## Ragi (finger millet): drought stress

Ragi tolerates dry spells better than paddy but shows stress as leaf rolling, dull blue-green
colour, and slower growth. Dig a small pit to check soil moisture at 15 cm depth, and record
whether the crop is at the vegetative or flowering stage. Flowering-stage drought causes the
largest yield loss. Ask about the date of sowing, the number of protective irrigations already
given, and whether the farmer has access to a borewell or tank.

## Sugarcane: waterlogging and root rot

In waterlogged sugarcane the lower leaves yellow and dry, the cane becomes soft at the base,
and a sour smell may be present. Check the low-lying patches and drainage channels first,
because waterlogging is rarely uniform across the field. Record the number of days the field
was under water, and whether the crop is at the formative or grand-growth stage. Suggest
opening drainage furrows and avoiding further irrigation until the soil drains.

## Maize: fall armyworm

Fall armyworm larvae feed in the whorl, leaving ragged holes and moist sawdust-like frass.
Inspect 10 plants per field by opening the whorl; record the percentage of plants with live
larvae and their size class. Small larvae are easier to manage than large ones. Ask the
farmer whether the field was scouted in the previous week, and whether neighbouring maize
fields show similar damage.

## What to record on every inspection

1. Village, crop, variety if known, and sowing date.
2. Percentage of plants affected and the area affected in hectares (measured or estimated).
3. Growth stage of the crop.
4. Rainfall and irrigation history since the last visit.
5. Photographs of the affected patch, taken from a fixed position where possible.
6. Advice given, and the date of the follow-up visit.

## Asking about affected area

When a farmer reports a problem, ask for the approximate area affected in hectares, or ask
for the number of guntas/acre and convert. If the farmer is unsure, estimate by walking the
affected patch and pacing its length and width. An unreliable affected-area figure is worse
than a clearly labelled estimate, so mark estimates as estimates.
